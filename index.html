I²C

Accelerometer and bus timing

The sensor. An FPGA I²C master driven from Python over FrontPanel, talks to the accelerometer on bus 1: configure it, burst-read X, Y, Z, convert to g, and check against gravity (at rest |a| = 1 g in any orientation).

The bus. Time the SDA rising edge on both I²C buses with a scope and turn it into each bus's capacitance through the 10 kΩ pull-up, then the max SCL each bus supports.

Section 0

Hardware: a new device on a different bus

The LSM303DLHC (U5) is two I²C devices in one package. It sits on bus 1, not the bus 0 your Lab 5 temperature sensor used. Remap SCL/SDA to I2C_SCL_1 / I2C_SDA_1 in xem7310_v1_fixed.xdc.

An I²C address byte is the 7-bit address shifted left by one, with the R/W bit in bit 0. So 0x19 becomes 0x32 to write and 0x33 to read.
Section 1

What to change in your I²C master

  1. Device address becomes a parameter or input: 0x19 (accel) or 0x1E (mag).
  2. Support the three transactions below.
  3. Make the auto-increment bit (top bit of the register address) a switch Python controls.
  4. Return all 6 bytes to the host through FrontPanel wires or a pipe.
START / Sr / STOP byte the FPGA sends byte the sensor sends ACK from sensor ACK/NACK from FPGA

Sr = repeated START: switch from writing the register address to reading, without releasing the bus. The last byte read gets a NACK, which tells the sensor to stop sending.

The register-address byte (0xA8)

One byte, two jobs. Bit 7 is a flag, not part of the address. Bits 6–0 are the first register of the burst. You send it once; the sensor moves its own pointer.

Section 8

Two buses, two loads

Same pull-ups, different number of devices. More devices means more capacitance, and more capacitance means a slower rising edge. That extra capacitance is the only difference you are measuring.

Each line stands for the SCL + SDA pair. Every line has its own 10.00 kΩ pull-up to 3.3 V: R4–R7 on the schematic.

Section 9

Why the rising edge is slow

I²C lines are open-drain: a device can only pull a line low. Nothing drives it high. When the line is released, the pull-up charges the bus capacitance through R, an RC curve. The fall is a transistor pulling hard (low resistance) to ground, so it is short.

One SCL bit = four ticks

  
The FSM runs on a tick 4× faster than SCL. Each bit walks through the same four steps.

Section 12

  
PMOD and the scope marker

  
PMOD (Peripheral Module) is Digilent's standard 12-pin expansion header: 8 I/O pins, 2 GND, 2 × 3.3 V. The sensor board has two, J1 (PMOD_A) and J2 (PMOD_B), wired straight to FPGA pins. In Lab 7 one I/O became a marker: a pin that is high only while the FSM is in one chosen state.

  

    
  

  
Pin 10 sits next to GND 11 the same way. Whichever I/O you used, ground the scope on the GND beside it.


  
The marker is one comparison, registered

  

    
  


always @(posedge clk) marker <= (state == TX_BYTE) && (byte_idx == 2) && (bit_cnt == 0);
assign pmod_marker = marker;   // tied to a PMOD pin in the .xdc

  
Register the comparison result, not the inputs, so the pin can't glitch.

Motor

  
DC motor and tilt angle

  
The motor has no position feedback here: N pulses only set how long it runs, not how far it turns. Tilt the rig and the accelerometer on the arm reads the real angle from gravity. Run, stop, read the angle.







  
Section 0

  
A pulse is drive time, not an angle

  
The motor is a 6 V brushed DC gearmotor. The FPGA sends pulses on EN1 at 200 Hz, so N pulses run the motor for N ÷ 200 s. How far the arm turns in that time depends on the supply voltage and the load.

  

  

    
  

  
Same pulses, different angle. Only the sensor knows where the arm is.

  
Why a gearmotor:

  
    
    
  • Spinning fast is efficient. Faster spin means a bigger back-EMF and less current is lost as heat.
    • 
    
  • The gearbox trades speed for torque. 
    • 
    
  • The arm needs slow speed and large torque. Torque is turning force: force × lever arm, in N·m.
    • 
  







  
Section 1

  
The rig, and where home goes

  
The arm is the board assembly itself, standing upright on the shaft. About 300 g turns on the shaft, balanced, with the IMU about 10 cm out. It travels about 300° between hard stops.

  

    

    
    

  

  
The motor won't notice a stop. Check that the arm moves, and cut the current if it doesn't, so the current doesn't turn into heat.

    •

Section 4

  
The driver: disable before you change direction

  
The Pmod DHB1 carries a DRV8833 dual H-bridge on a PMOD header. The gearmotor has two wires, so it uses one bridge, motor 1, with two pins: EN1 and DIR1. Motor power comes from a bench supply, 6 V at most.

  
    
    
  • EN1: on/off. The 200 Hz pulses go here, and EN1 low stops it (coast).
    • 
    
  • DIR1: which way it turns. Change it only while EN1 is low.
    • 
  

  

    

    
    

  

  

    

      
Flip DIR1 only while EN1 is low (all switches open).

      
Flip while enabled causes a brief short through the FETs.

    

  

  
Flyback. The motor winding is an inductor: switch it off fast and it makes whatever voltage keeps its current flowing, thousands of volts. The DRV8833 gives that current a path through its body diodes and decay modes.

Section 8

  
Hysteresis

  
After a reversal, the motor has to turn a little extra before the arm moves (backlash: slack in the gearbox, up to 3°). So where the arm stops depends on which way it came from (hysteresis).

  

    

  

  
Dot (pin): turned by the motor. It moves the moment the motor moves.
Outline (slot): the arm's side. It only moves when the pin pushes on one of its walls.
Shaded space: the gap, which is the backlash.

  
Mechanical, not electrical. It comes from the gears, plus a little static friction. The motor's electrical side has no memory of position.











  
Camera

  
Image sensor timing

  
The CMV300 is a 648 × 488 global-shutter image sensor. This lab never stores a pixel. The FPGA powers the sensor up in order, sets it over SPI and asks for frames, and counters inside the FPGA prove each number: the clock, the rows, the pixels and the frame timing.







  
Section 0

  
Three ways to talk to the sensor

  
The sensor connects to the FPGA in three separate ways. SPI only sets it up; the frame itself comes out on the video bus.

  

    

    
    

  







  
Section 1

  
One frame: exposure, FOT, readout

  
The sensor has a global shutter: every pixel starts and stops collecting light at the same moment. A frame then runs in three phases:

  
    
    
  • Exposure: all pixels collect light at once. You pick how long, like a camera's shutter speed.
    • 
    
  • FOT (frame overhead time): the whole image moves into storage for readout, also at once.
    • 
    
  • Readout: the slow part. Every pixel leaves on one 10-bit bus, one per clock, row by row.
    • 
  

  

    
  

  
One frame from idle, to scale, at the default exposure.

  

  







  
Section 2

  
SPI: the sensor is a set of registers

  
Before it's a camera, the CMV300 is a register file behind a four-wire SPI port. One transfer is 16 bits, MSB first: a read/write bit, a 7-bit address and a data byte. Run the clock at 10 MHz or less.

  

    

    
    

  

  
On each data line, one side changes the bit and the other samples it:

  
    
    
  • SPI_IN: the FPGA changes it, the sensor samples it.
    • 
    
  • SPI_OUT: the sensor changes it, the FPGA samples it.
    • 
  

  

    
  

  
SPI_EN stays high for the whole transfer.
Both data lines change on the falling edge and are read on the rising edge.







  
Section 3

  
Two registers make one number

  
Settings wider than 8 bits span two registers, low byte first.

  

  





  
Section 4

  
Power-up is a sequence, not a switch

  
Your sequencer brings the sensor up in this order. Get it wrong and nothing reports it: the sensor just doesn't answer.

  

    

  
  
sensor_ready is your bit. The sensor never says it's ready. Reading the register defaults over SPI confirms it.

  
Why hardware. Python can't time 1 µs; a counter in the FPGA can.

  
Going down: hold reset low with the clock still running, then stop the clock.

Section 6

  
FRAME_REQ: a command with no reply

  
FRAME_REQ means "take a frame now". Unlike an I²C or SPI command, it gets no reply: the only sign it worked is video on other pins, milliseconds later. So you check what follows:

Section 7

  
Two timing regimes

  
The same registers give two frame rates, depending on how you ask. One frame from idle runs its phases strictly in order. With Number_frames above 1, or FRAME_REQ held high, the next exposure overlaps the current readout.

  

    

  

  

  







  
Section 8

  
What one row looks like

  
The video bus has three parts, sampled on the falling edge of CLK_OUT:

  
    
    
  • D[9:0]: one pixel's brightness, 10 bits, 0 to 1023.
    • 
    
  • LVAL (line valid): high for one row. Its rising edges count rows.
    • 
    
  • DVAL (data valid): high when D holds a real pixel.

Takeaways

  
What carries over to other hardware

  
Habits and patterns from these labs that apply to most embedded and FPGA work, whatever the chip.






  
Section 0

  
Test a known answer

  
Before trusting any measurement from a new chip, read something whose value you already know. If that comes back right, the wiring, the bus, the timing and the bit order all work. If it doesn't, nothing after it can be trusted.

  
    
    
  • ID register: many chips have one that always reads a fixed value listed in the datasheet.
    • 
    
  • Reset defaults: registers start at documented values after reset.
    • 
    
  • Write, then read back: write a value, read it again, compare.
    • 
  

  
Example. Write 0x5A and read back 0xB4: every bit moved one place left, so the read samples one clock late.






  
Section 1

  
Power up in order

  
A complex chip needs its supplies, then a running clock, then reset released, then its settings. Reset clears the registers, so settings written before it are lost, and a chip with no clock can't come out of reset cleanly. A PLL also needs time to lock before its clock can be used.

  
    
    
  • Supplies stable → clock on → hold reset → release reset → wait → configure → wait for lock or ready → start.
    • 
    
  • The datasheet gives this as a timing diagram. Every wait in it is a minimum.
    • 
    
  • Getting it wrong rarely gives an error. The chip just doesn't answer, or answers with nonsense.
    • 
  






  
Section 2

  
Don't trust defaults

  
A default suits a typical case, not necessarily your board, and datasheets have typos that a later revision corrects. So read the revision history and the errata, and measure anything you only assumed: a clock frequency, a mode, a gain.

  
Example. A PLL set for the wrong input range may still lock, just at the wrong frequency. Everything runs and every time measurement is off by the same ratio. Only measuring the clock shows it.






  
Section 3

  
How SPI works

  
SPI has four wires: a clock and a chip select from the master, one data line out of the master and one back. Each transfer is a fixed number of bits, usually a read/write bit, an address and a data byte, sent MSB first.

  
On each data line one side changes the bit on one clock edge and the other side samples it on the opposite edge, so the bit has half a period to settle. To set up a master, read the slave's datasheet rather than guessing a mode number:

  
    
    
  • Find the edge the slave samples its input on. Change your output on the other edge.
    • 
    
  • Find the edge the slave changes its output on. Sample its output on the other edge.
    • 
  

  
The same pattern of a frame plus edges appears in flash memory, ADCs, DACs and most sensors.






  
Section 4

  
Multi-byte values

  
A value wider than 8 bits is stored in two or more 8-bit registers. The datasheet says which byte comes first: low byte first (little-endian) or high byte first (big-endian).

  
Example. 300 = 0x12C, stored as a low byte 0x2C and a high byte 0x01. Value = low + 256 × high. With the bytes swapped you'd get 0x2C01 = 11,265.

  
A one-bit shift in the receiver doubles or halves every value, which can look plausible. A known value is the only reliable check.






  
Section 5

  
Parallel buses

  
Fast data often comes out on several data lines at once, with its own clock and one or more "valid" lines that say when the data means something. Cameras, displays and fast ADCs use this shape.

  
    
    
  • Sample on the clock edge opposite the one where the data changes.
    • 
    
  • Count or store only while the valid line is high. Gaps can sit inside a line, not just between lines.
    • 
    
  • A long low stretch on a valid line usually marks the end of a block, such as a frame.
    • 
  






  
Section 6

  
Crossing clock domains

  
A flip-flop samples its input on a clock edge. If the input changes right at that edge, the flip-flop can hang between 0 and 1 for a while (metastability). That's unavoidable when a signal comes from logic on a different, unrelated clock.

  
    
    
  • One bit: pass it through two flip-flops in a row. The first may go metastable; the second gives it a full cycle to settle.
    • 
    
  • Many bits: two flip-flops per bit isn't enough. Each bit may be caught on a different cycle, so the value can read half old and half new.
    • 
    
  • The fix: hold the value still, flip one toggle bit, and send only that bit across. When the other side sees the toggle change, the value is stable, so it copies it.
    • 
  

  
Clock-crossing bugs are rare and random, so they're hard to find in testing. This is one of the most common FPGA interview topics.






  
Section 7

  
Measure with counters

  
A counter inside an FPGA can replace a scope. A counter counts events: rows, pixels, pulses. A meter counts clock ticks over a known time, which gives a frequency or a duration.

  
Example. Open a 1 ms window timed by a known 100 MHz clock (100,000 ticks) and count edges of an unknown clock during it. 25,000 edges means 25 MHz.

  
The same idea is behind built-in self-test in production hardware, which checks itself with no lab equipment.






  
Section 8

  
Hardware vs software timing

  
A sleep in software on a PC or an OS is coarse and varies run to run, often by a millisecond or more. A counter in hardware on a 100 MHz clock is exact to 10 ns.

  
    
    
  • Microsecond waits, or anything that must be exact: a hardware counter.
    • 
    
  • Millisecond waits with plenty of margin: software is fine.
    • 
  

  
Splitting work this way between firmware and logic is a decision you make on almost every board.






  
Section 9

  
Edge detection

  
Many inputs are meant as one-time commands but may arrive as a level that stays high. Pass it on as is and the receiver may act on it again and again. An edge detector gives exactly one clock-wide pulse per rising edge: keep last cycle's value and fire when the input is high now and was low before.

  
reg prev;
always @(posedge clk) prev <= in;
assign pulse = in & ~prev;   // high for one clock on each rising edge

  
If the input comes from another clock domain, bring it across with two flip-flops first.






  
Section 10

  
Check the effect

  
Some commands get no reply: a trigger pin, a start-conversion strobe, a pulse to a motor driver. Since there's nothing to read back, check the effect instead: data that arrives, a flag that rises, a delay you can time.

  
Example. After a "start" pulse, time how long until the first data appears and count how much arrives. Both should match what the datasheet predicts.






  
Section 11

  
Catch silent failures

  
Hardware usually fails quietly. Missing clocks, wrong settings and early commands each just produce nothing, or something slightly off. List every condition that must hold, and next to each one, the measurement that would show it doesn't.

  
    
    
  • Clock missing → a frequency counter reads 0.
    • 
    
  • A setting not written → a register read-back shows the default.
    • 
    
  • A command sent too early → the first result is missing or the wrong size.
    • 
  

  
That list is a bring-up checklist and a test plan.






  
Section 12

  
Find stuck lines

  
Keep a running OR and a running AND of every word on a bus. Start OR at all 0s and AND at all 1s.

  
    
    
  • A bit that never turns on in OR: that line never went high, so it's stuck low or open.
    • 
    
  • A bit that stays on in AND: that line never went low, so it's stuck high.
    • 
  

  
With varied data, OR should end all 1s and AND all 0s. This takes two registers per bus, and works on any bus: ADC data, address lines, memory.






  
Section 13

  
Predict, then measure

  
Work out the expected number, with a tolerance, before taking the measurement. If the two disagree, the gap tells you something: a wrong assumption, a wrong setting or a bug. A measurement with no prediction has nothing to disagree with.






  
Section 14

  
Pipelining

  
If a job has stages that run one after another, one item takes the sum of all stages. If the next item can start an early stage while the current one is still in a later stage, the stages overlap, and a new item finishes once per slowest stage.

  
Example. Washing takes 30 min and drying 40 min. One load takes 70 min. With many loads, one finishes every 40 min.

  
Overlap hides the faster stages but doesn't make the slowest one faster. Speeding up anything except the slowest stage changes nothing. The same holds for CPU pipelines, double-buffered DMA and streaming data.
